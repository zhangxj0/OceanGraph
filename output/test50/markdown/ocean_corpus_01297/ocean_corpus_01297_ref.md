## Abstract

Increasing hydrostatic pressure induces the release of dissolved organic matter from rapidly settling marine particles and contributes to the depth attenuation of carbon fluxes.

The ocean is a major sink of carbon and governs atmospheric carbon dioxide levels on timescales of tens to thousands of years. This important role is related to the large volume and surface area of the ocean and the reaction of carbon dioxide with water that eventually results in bicarbonate and carbonate ions that do not exchange directly with the atmosphere. The partitioning of carbon between a well-mixed ocean and the atmosphere would result in a preindustrial atmospheric carbon dioxide level about double the value inferred from ice-core studies. However, ocean mixing is sluggish, and there is a substantial gradient in dissolved inorganic carbon between the ocean’s surface and deeper waters. Because of this gradient, ocean carbon storage is about 10% higher than it would be in a homogenous ocean, and atmospheric carbon dioxide is two times lower, consistent with preindustrial carbon dioxide values (1).

This gradient in dissolved inorganic carbon is maintained by carbon pumps (2), particularly the solubility and biological pumps that account for one-third and two-thirds of the dissolved inorganic carbon gradient, respectively. The biological carbon pump (BCP) refers to an umbrella of processes (primary production, transfer of organic and inorganic carbon particles to the ocean interior, degradation of organic particles, and dissolution of calcium carbonates) that cause a net transfer of carbon from the surface to the deep and dissolved inorganic carbon enrichment of the ocean’s deep waters. The BCP moves about 10 Pg of C year−1 into deeper waters by gravitational settling particles, vertically migrating organisms, and physical mixing. The gravitational pump accounts for 70% of the vertical carbon transfer and acts over the full ocean depth, while vertical transport due to migrating animals and physical mixing is limited to the upper kilometer (3). The depth to which carbon is transferred is a key factor in governing carbon sequestration time (4).

The carbon settling flux is attenuated with depth because of various processes, including zooplankton consumption, microbial degradation, solubilization to dissolved organic carbon by particle-attached microbes, carbonate mineral dissolution, and alteration in sinking speed, a result of particle aggregation and disaggregation. Environmental factors such as oxygen and temperature have been identified as key factors governing particulate carbon attenuation with depth (4–6), but hydrostatic pressure, increasing with depth to about 49 MPa at 5 km, has so far been neglected for the BCP’s organic, soft-tissue component. Pressure is a key factor governing the BCP’s carbonate component because it affects carbonate equilibria and calcium carbonate mineral solubility with the result that calcium carbonate skeletons dissolve at depth in the ocean.

In this issue of Science Advances, Stief et al. (7) report experimental evidence showing that sinking diatom aggregates exposed to increasing hydrostatic pressure leak dissolved organic matter (DOM) and demonstrate that this organic matter is labile and readily consumed by microbes. Although hydrostatic pressure has been recognized as an environmental factor governing deep-sea microbes more than four decades ago (8), experimental challenges have limited progress.

Decompression of deep-sea communities leads to DOM release due to cell lysis, and it is for this reason that sediment biogeochemists use in situ profiling and incubation techniques for flux determinations. Settling particles are subject to increasing hydrostatic pressure (about 1 MPa per 100 m), and pressure-controlled experiments have revealed impacts on enzymatic activities, oxygen consumption rates, and microbial community composition (9).

Stief et al. (7) show that rapidly sinking diatom aggregates release DOM at hydrostatic pressures corresponding to water depths more than 2 km. Pressure-exposed diatoms leak intracellular solutes, both from the cytoplasm and vacuoles, into the surrounding waters, likely because of cell-membrane integrity loss. The released DOM is carbohydrate and protein rich. Ultrahigh-resolution mass spectrometry revealed the released DOM to be labile. Stief et al. (7) demonstrate that surface microbial communities can use the pressure-induced released DOM for respiration and for producing microbial biomass and new dissolved organic compounds, indicating that the pressure-released DOM may enter the microbial loop.

If deep-sea microbes have similar capabilities, pressure-induced DOM leakage will then represent a new pathway of labile carbon supply to the deep-sea microbial communities, in addition to viral lysis and enzymatic solubilization of particulate organic matter. This pressure-induced released DOM may fuel a deep-sea plankton food web and affect microbial community composition and functioning. The released intracellular compounds DOM can serve as priming substrates to stimulate additional degradation of recalcitrant deep-sea DOM. However, microbial processing and transformation of pressure-induced released DOM may also result in the formation of recalcitrant DOM components that persist and contribute to the microbial carbon pump (10). Pressure-induced released DOM might thus link the gravitational biological carbon and microbial carbon pumps. The net balance between microbial production of recalcitrant DOM and priming-induced additional consumption of recalcitrant DOM is unknown but critical to evaluate the impact of pressure on the microbial carbon pump.

This pressure-induced DOM leakage represents a new pathway for attenuating particulate carbon fluxes. However, its importance relative to respiration by particle-attached microbes and zooplankton grazing remains to be established. Pressure-induced DOM leakage is likely not important to carbon flux attenuation in the upper ocean where zooplankton grazing dominates (5, 6) but may be important at depths more than 2 km. Pressure-induced carbon flux attenuation results in less particulate organic matter eventually reaching deep-sea sediments. However, this newly identified carbon flux attenuation pathway will probably not have a major impact on oceans’ carbon sequestration. Why? Pressure-induced DOM leakage happens at water depths with sequestration times of more than 500 years (4), and the specific form in which carbon is sequestered (dissolved inorganic, dissolved organic, or particulate carbon) is then of lesser importance.

Pressure-induced leakage of DOM requires rapid settling of primary producers and their aggregates, e.g., as marine snow (Fig. 1), and thus depends on surface water phytoplankton community composition. The experiments were performed with the diatom Skeletonema marinol and a daily pressure increase of 5 MPa, corresponding to a rather high aggregate sinking velocity of 500 m day−1. It is unclear whether slower aggregate sinking velocities would generate a similar pressure-induced leakage pattern. Incubations with three other diatom species, including centric and pennate diatoms, generated consistent results, suggesting that the reported hydrostatic pressure–induced DOM leakage is widespread among diatoms. The next steps are to investigate whether other marine-snow forming phytoplankton such as dinoflagellates and prymnesiophytes (coccolithophores and Phaeocystis) show pressure-induced DOM leakage as well and whether prokaryotic primary producers (cyanobacteria) with different membrane architecture show similar sensitivity to hydrostatic pressure.
