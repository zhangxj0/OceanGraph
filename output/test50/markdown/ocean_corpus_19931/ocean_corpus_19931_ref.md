observed high saturation of methane in the surface ocean.2 However, these
exogenous sources account for a minor fraction of surface methane supersaturation in pelagic waters with greater depths.

marine algae are capable of producing methane using bicarbonate or methylated sulfur compounds as a precursor under oxygenated conditions.
Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that cyanobacteria are important
contributors for methane production via multiple mechanisms in aquatic
environments.3 Cyanobacteria that can fix nitrogen have the capability of
phosphonate metabolism; the demethylation of methylphosphonate generates methane as a byproduct. Diverse nitrogen-fixing bacteria from different
environments have been documented to reduce carbon dioxide simultaneously with nitrogen gas to yield methane when expressing iron-iron nitrogenase.3 Considering that nitrogenase is an ATP-requiring enzyme prevalent in
nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria, it is plausible that ATP generated during
photosynthesis could drive the enzymatic conversion of carbon dioxide to
methane. In addition, cyanobacteria living in marine, freshwater, and terrestrial environments can convert fixed inorganic carbon into methane under
light conditions at substantial rates, which further highlights the role of
photoautotrophs in methane production.
In addition to biological production, abiotic photoproduction from chromophoric dissolved organic matter (CDOM) also contributes to the methane
efflux in the open ocean.3 Methane derived from the photodegradation of
CDOM could account for approximately 20–60% of the open-ocean sea-air
methane emission fluxes, indicating that other methane production mechanisms exist to support oceanic methane supersaturation. More recently, a
novel non-enzymatic methane formation pathway has been proposed in all
living organisms.5 This process is triggered by reactive oxygen species (ROS)
and free iron, with methylated sulfur or nitrogen compounds as intermediates. ROS are highly reactive metabolites of oxygen that are produced during
aerobic metabolism in cells; this is a ubiquitous process in the ocean. Since
ROS could be toxic to living cells, marine organisms have developed antioxidant systems to prevent oxidative stress. In particular, DMSP released by
phytoplankton can serve as an antioxidant that can scavenge hydroxyl radicals and other ROS. Hence, it is reasonable to expect that methane synthesis
from ROS-driven demethylation of sulfur-methyl compounds may occur in
the ocean. However, current investigations on this pathway are limited to
laboratory experiments with stressed conditions, while the environmental
relevance and significance of methane production remain uncertain in nature.
CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES
In global oceans, microbial growth is often limited by nutrient availability
(e.g., N, P, Fe). To cope with P-limiting conditions, microbes have evolved
specific strategies to degrade dissolved organic phosphorus (DOP) and
acquire inorganic P for cell growth. Particularly, organic phosphorus
compounds containing C-P bonds such as methylphosphonate (MPn) are
readily used and the decomposition of these compounds through C-P lyase
can release methane as a byproduct.2 Since the discovery of this mechanism
of oxic methanogenesis, increasing evidence suggests that this pathway is
prevalent in marine waters. Methane production was always observed in
incubation experiments following amendment of MPn to coastal or oceanic
waters. Metagenomic analyses revealed that key genes associated with MPn
biosynthesis, such as phophoenolpyruvate mutase gene (pepM) and
methylphosphonate synthase (MPnS), are ubiquitous among marine
microbes globally.1-3 Furthermore, microbes catabolizing MPn through C-P
lyase pathway, which is encoded by a 14 gene operon (phnC–phnP) induced
by the pho regulon under phosphate starvation, have been shown to be
widespread across the global ocean. Although the concentrations of MPn in
seawater were barely reported, phosphonate esters are abundant in the
ocean as they are important constituents of dissolved organic matter.1
Bacterial degradation of polysaccharide esters of phosphonic acids in
dissolved organic matter (DOM) can also release methane, and daily cycling
of only a minor fraction of semi-labile DOM polysaccharide-MPn esters can
support observed sea-air methane fluxes.
Collectively, marine methane production, previously attributed solely to
anaerobic processes, is now being recognized as a multifaceted puzzle
involving multiple aerobic biological, biochemical and abiotic pathways. The
emerging evidence challenges traditional notions and provides alternative
explanations for the methane paradox. Although these pathways are still
being refined, what emerges is a picture of concurrent and parallel processes
contributing to the pervasive supersaturation of methane in oxic marine
waters (Figure 1). To better understand these newly discovered pathways, it
is important to identify the biochemistry and the microorganisms involved
through robust laboratory and field work. Given the large spatio-temporal
variation in microbiology and biogeochemistry among oceanic environments,
the magnitude and contribution of each pathway for methane production
could be different and require further investigation. Therefore, future research
should revisit these oxic methane sources across marine systems, with a
focus on the practical possibilities and specific contribution of each identified
pathway under in situ conditions. A better understanding of the physiological
and environmental controls of individual methanogenesis pathways will
advance the knowledge of the long-term impact of the environmental
changes (e.g., algal blooms, global warming, elevated ROS) on the aerobic
production of methane. Ultimately, the methane production under changes
should be linked to the oceanic emissions of methane to the atmosphere,
which influences the global methane budget and potential climate feedbacks.

In addition to the C-P lyase from phosphonate, aerobic degradation of
other methyl compounds such as dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) or
methylamines could be linked to methane production in aquatic environments. DMSP or methylamines are osmolyte compounds produced by
marine biota and play important roles in microbial ecology and seawater
chemistry. It is well documented that DMSP or methylamines constitute
energy-rich substrates for methanogens in marine sediments. In the upper
ocean, direct evidence for DMSP-induced methane production came from the
observed methane formation after the addition of DMSP to water samples or
the occasional correlations between in situ methane and methylated sulfur
compound concentrations.2,3 However, the detailed mechanism for this
process is quite enigmatic, as the microorganisms, biochemistry or genes
involved in this pathway remain unclear. Similarly, microbial conversion of
methylamine to methane under aerobic conditions has been reported in oxic
lakes.4 The critical gene that encodes pyridoxylamine phosphate-dependent
aspartate aminotransferase has been identified for the aerobic bacterial
methane synthesis from methylamine. While these findings underscore the
potential of methylamine as a methanogenic substrate in aquatic systems,
evidence is required for the occurrence of methylamine-driven methanogenesis in marine waters. In contrast to the high abundance of methylamine in
freshwater lakes, considerably lower concentrations of methylamine in the
ocean and the fact that methylamine serves primarily as an energy source for
methylotrophs, may significantly limit the conversion of methylamine to
methane. Therefore, while this pathway remains possible in marine environments, current evidence suggests that if this process occurs, it may proceed
