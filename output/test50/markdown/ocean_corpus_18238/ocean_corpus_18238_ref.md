# Climate change and marine ecosystems: Implications for human health

Despite serious progress in the development of nonconventional energy resources, fossil fuel continues to remain the most convenient source of energy across the globe. Ever-increasing usage of fossil fuels has resulted in a significant increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air, which has resulted in global warming and climate change. While the degree of warming varies across various geographical locations, there is a consensus that the net effect of increasing CO 2 emissions is that of warming. Another effect is the increased dissolution of CO 2 in sea water resulting in ocean acidification. This has dire consequences for marine life, especially to the calcareous shell-forming organisms such as molluskans. There is also clear-cut evidence that the harmful algal blooms (HABs) are increasing in frequency and intensity, leading to massive fish kills and entry of potential toxins to the food chain, endangering even human life. Warming trends in the subtropics and near-polar regions also favor mesophilic pathogens to extend their range to these regions. The characteristic feature of climate change is the frequent occurrence of extreme weather events. It is a sobering thought that the countries affected, both developed and developing, do not have the wherewithal to fight against the powers of nature and are in danger of incurring losses, both economic and lives.

## C limate C hange and M arine E cosystems

While the increasing CO 2 emissions are resulting in an increase in the global surface temperatures, it also extends to the waters of the world. Oceans being highly dynamic, act like giant conveyor belts taking warm waters from the tropics to the polar regions, and returning with cold waters from there to the tropics. The overall raised temperatures are reflected in these movements, especially in the resultant greater melting of the polar ice, thus raising sea levels. This has been assessed in a systematic way by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. It has published four major assessment reports since its establishment in 1988. The future sea-level rises projected in the first to fourth assessment reports (1990, 1995, 2001, and 2007) are 31–110 cm (business as usual scenario), 13–94 cm, 9–88 cm, and 18–59 cm, respectively. [ ]

Although this seems as a minimal increase, the consequences are quite significant. The Arctic Ocean, which is now warming at a faster pace, has lost most of its sea ice. While this augurs well for some of the species as the loss of ice cover increases productivity by admitting more light into the polar waters, it is an impending threat to the keystone species such as the polar bear. The polar bears move on the sea ice and hunt at the seal holes. The fast disappearance of both the latter entities is resulting in a loss of hunting grounds for polar bears, and they are facing an existential threat.

As far as human health is concerned, the implications of rising sea level on human health are multipronged. The coastal regions across the globe are the most populated areas and support more than 50% of the population. [ ] These regions, especially in developing and third-world countries, do not have the infrastructure to treat the ever-increasing quantum of sewage released into the coastal water, either untreated or partially treated. More than 80% of wastewater is directly disposed untreated into rivers or the ocean waters, causing detrimental effect on the water quality along the coast. [ ] The quantum of wastewater discharged into these water bodies is greatly increased during the rainstorm events.

Inundation of the coastal regions due to the advancing sea-level results in malfunctioning of the septic tanks as well as providing an ideal environment for the mixing of waste water-borne pathogens with that of drinking water sources such as wells. This also provides an ideal habitat for horizontal gene transfer among pathogens from various sources leading to the evolution of multidrug-resistant pathogens. Our previous studies revealed considerable infiltration of sewage-borne bacteria into well water sources as well as evolution of hypervirulent strains of Escherichia coli from the coastal regions. [ , ] Saltwater intrusion into the drinking water sources along the coastal regions also poses a considerable risk to human health. Several of the coastal regions do not have piped water supply and rely on well water sources at their homes. The high salt content/hardness of this water poses a health risk to the consumers. There are also reports that warming trend due to climate change is favoring the spread of mesophilic pathogens as they extend their geographical spread. There are published reports on increased incidences of malaria and other mosquito-borne diseases. The increasing CO 2 concentrations in the air also get into the water turning the seawater acidic. This ocean acidification is a major threat to the calcareous shell-forming organisms such as molluskans as the dissolution of calcium carbonate takes place faster in an acidic environment. Another major consequence of warming oceans is the increasing frequency of harmful algal blooms (HABs). The frequency of HABs is increasing along the coastal regions across the globe. This is depicted in Figure 3 . Several harmful algae such as Noctiluca , Gymnodinium , and Alexandrium are increasing in numbers in warm sea water resulting in massive fish kills. This includes paralytic shellfish poisoning, diarrhetic shellfish poisoning, neuretic shellfish poisoning, and amnesic shellfish poisoning. The potent toxins accumulated in filter-feeding organisms such as bivalve shellfish will ultimately get into the human food chain causing serious health effects to the consumer.

Global warming and resultant climate change on terrestrial and marine ecosystems spell doom and an existential threat to humanity. Because the oceans cover more than 70% of planet Earth, it could be considered as planet ocean and the consequences resulting from the climate change in marine ecosystems are rather significant. It seriously compromises our food security as the coastal populations across the world are depending on fish for their consumption and economic growth; serious perturbations in the marine ecosystems will cost them dear. Besides threat to food security, other issues such as loss of lives, loss of property, inability to engage in livelihood fishing and resultant poverty are also of serious concerns. HABs and diseases caused by pathogenic vibrios are also posing a serious challenge to human health.

In this context, it is our prime duty to reduce our ecological footprint and resort to consumption patterns and developmental paradigms, which are in sync with nature. Humanity can sustain and survive only by being part of nature. Let us strive harder to achieve this objective and continue to live peacefully and harmoniously on this beautiful earth, comprising both land and water.
