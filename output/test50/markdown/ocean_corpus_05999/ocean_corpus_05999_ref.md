INTRODUCTION & SCOPE OF WORK
Anthropogenic CO2 emissions are drastically changing the global climate, with multiple
consequences such as rising temperatures and ocean acidification (IPCC, 2023). These
widespread changes are negatively impacting biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, and the
communities that rely on them, prompting efforts to reduce current CO2 emissions (Zawude
Bakure et al., 2022; Campbell-Lendrum et al., 2023).
One proposed method for reducing CO2 emissions and mitigating climate change is carbon
capture and storage (CCS), a technology that involves injecting concentrated CO2 into suitable
geological reservoirs for long-term storage (Roy et al., 2023; Eyitayo et al., 2025). These
reservoirs are often located sub-seafloor in coastal areas, which have raised concerns over
potential impacts on the marine environment (Damen et al., 2006). For instance, leakage of CO2
and brine, although unlikely, may affect local ecosystems, while the establishment of connected
infrastructures may affect biodiversity on a wider scale (Blackford et al., 2009; Rastelli et al.,
2016).
COREu is a pioneering Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) project demonstrating key
technologies at sites in southern and central Europe. One aim is to demonstrate full-scale CCS
beneath the Prinos oil field, located in the Kavala Gulf in Greece. Although most research
suggests CO2 leakages may have minor, local and reversible environmental impacts, collection
of robust baseline data is still essential for detecting potential changes in biodiversity. Therefore,
a central task in COREu is to develop and demonstrate an innovative method for collection of
detailed baseline data describing the marine environment prior to CCS activities, using analyses
of environmental DNA (eDNA) as a sensitive and non-invasive monitoring tool.
METHODOLOGY
To establish a robust biodiversity baseline prior to CCS activities, sediment and water samples
were collected three times over one year (summer, autumn and winter) to capture seasonal
variation in biodiversity and eDNA. Sampling was carried out along a 22 km transect following
the oil pipeline from the shore to beyond the planned CO2 storage site beneath the oil extraction
facilities, capturing nearshore-offshore sediment gradients and areas potentially affected by CCS
activities (Figure 1a).

Environmental DNA from all samples is stored in NINAs biobank in Norway. If a CO₂ leak occurs
at some point in the future, new samples can be collected after the leakage and compared with
the archived baseline material. Targeted analyses using state-of-the-art genetic methods can
then reveal if, and to what extent, adverse environmental impacts may have occurred.
To demonstrate the applicability of using eDNA as baseline biodiversity data, a subset of the
samples was processed using high‑throughput metabarcoding (Figure 1b). The assessments
were performed using eDNA metabarcoding focused on benthic macroinvertebrates,
Foraminifera and fish species. Both macroinvertebrates and Foraminifera (small, single-celled
organisms) have been widely used as bioindicators of human impacts on ecosystems.
Foraminifera are particularly useful for CCS monitoring, as they respond quickly to environmental
changes such as potential CO2 leakages. Conventional taxonomical analyses of Foraminifera
were also performed to validate the representativeness of the eDNA results.

RESEARCH OUTCOMES
All nature‑based industries can produce undesired or unexpected impacts on biodiversity and
ecosystems, yet such effects are often difficult to assess without robust baseline data. Even
when baseline data exist, they may not adequately capture the spatiotemporal variability in
biodiversity needed to reliably detect ecological change. Conventional methods for collecting
biological baseline data may also have limited value for assessing unforeseen impacts that arise
years or even decades later. As analytical technologies, including artificial intelligence, continue
to advance, the ability to revisit and analyze preserved material becomes increasingly important.
One aim of the COREu project was therefore to develop and demonstrate an innovative
eDNA‑based approach to biological baseline data collection that addresses the limitations of
conventional methods. The sampling design was structured to account for potential
spatiotemporal variability in biodiversity when comparing baseline and post‑impact data. In
addition, the long‑term storage of eDNA from both sediment and water samples enables future
re‑analysis using state‑of‑the‑art technologies. Because eDNA contains genetic material from a
wide range of organisms, the COREu approach also allows retrospective analyses targeting newly
identified species of concern, an option often unavailable through conventional baseline
methods.
In conclusion, the COREu approach tackles major challenges in biological baseline monitoring
through a seasonal and spatially extensive sampling design capable of detecting both localized
short‑term effects and broader long‑term regional impacts. By preserving eDNA from sediments
and water the approach is effectively future‑proof, allowing material to be revisited with more
advanced methods as new environmental concerns emerge. This makes the COREu approach

broadly applicable for detecting ecological effects from multiple human pressures and a strong
complement to traditional monitoring as molecular and AI‑driven tools continue to advance.
